Case study

Four tables, three tabs

Redesigning how Novax's school health module shows a child's vision, hearing, and language screening history — replacing four dense tables stacked on one page with three focused tabs built around what a health visitor needs first: the latest result, and whether it needs following up.

Role Digital Designer
Company Novax A/S
Timeline Summer 2026
Tools Figma, AI-assisted iteration
Overview

Three test types, buried in one page

The old version of this module put every screening result a child had ever had onto a single page: one table for vision tests, two more for hearing — one per ear — and a fourth for language assessments. Finding the most recent result meant scanning a wide table for the newest date; judging whether it needed attention meant reading every column by hand. The data wasn't wrong, it just wasn't organised around the two questions a health visitor actually asks first: what's the latest measurement, and does it need follow-up?

My task was to redesign the module around those two questions for three quite different kinds of data — a handful of values per eye, up to 22 measurement points across both ears, and a short qualitative language note — without losing any of the detail the old tables held.

Process

Deciding what earns a glance

I split the page into three tabs — Synsprøver, Høreprøver, and Sprogvurderinger — one per test type, so each could use whatever layout actually fit its data instead of three different shapes forced into one table. Before settling on those layouts, I used an AI as a sparring partner: describing the data and the reading task, asking for alternative ways to lay it out, then iterating on its suggestions rather than taking any of them as-is.

Two patterns came out of that process and carried across the vision and hearing tabs. A summary card for the latest measurement sits above the history table, showing the newest result and its change from the previous one without opening anything — language assessments have too few fields to need one, so that tab stays a plain list instead. And anything worth a second look is marked before you have to go looking for it: a value outside the normal range gets a warning colour wherever it appears, and any measurement flagged with an anmærkning — a note asking for follow-up — adds to a small running count at the top of the page, linking straight to the row it belongs to.

A vision card, a hearing summary, a language note — three different shapes, built to answer the same question first: does this result need a second look?
Solution

Three tabs, three shapes

What each tab actually shows past that shared foundation depends entirely on its data — a handful of numbers, more than twenty of them, or almost none.

Synsprøver

Some municipalities screen with a Snellen chart, others with LogMAR, and health visitors switch between the two depending on which school they're visiting — so a button group lets them pick the active scale, and both "Ny synsprøve" and "Registrer normalt syn" write using whichever one is selected. In the history table, working with AI on the column layout got each eye's reading down to one cell: the corrected measurement if the child wears glasses or lenses, since that's what confirms the prescription is still right, otherwise the uncorrected one. The other value doesn't disappear — it drops into the row's expanded detail, still there for tracking whether vision is drifting over time even when it isn't the number that matters most today.

The two states below are the same card responding to different children: one with a prescription to confirm, one without a corrected value to show at all — and flagged for follow-up on top of that.

Høreprøver

A single hearing test produces up to 22 measurements — eleven frequencies per ear. Read as a raw table, even a completely normal result takes real effort to confirm. Working through it with AI, I landed on two numbers per ear instead: the worst measurement recorded, and how many of the eleven frequencies fall outside the normal range. A normal result becomes obvious at a glance — worst value in range, nothing flagged — and so does a result worth a closer look, along with roughly how much of it is affected.

Seeing which frequencies are the problem — low tones, high tones, one ear or both — means expanding the row into the full reading: the same numbers, plotted on a chart shaped like a clinical audiogram, so a health visitor can see exactly where a child's hearing drops off instead of reading it out of a column of decibels.

Sprogvurderinger

Language assessments are the simplest of the three: a date and the responsible health visitor identify the entry, a yes/no judgement and a short free-text note describe it, and an optional anmærkning flags it for follow-up — shown only on entries that actually carry one. Five fields, most of them text, is exactly the case a plain list handles better than a table built for numbers.

Outcome

What carries forward

The layouts here aren't ones I'd have reached alone on a first pass. Sparring with AI didn't hand me the final design — it gave me a spread of different ways to shape the same data, and iterating against them made it obvious which questions I hadn't actually answered yet, like what a normal hearing result should look like at a glance next to one that needs a second look. What I'd carry forward isn't a specific layout so much as that process, and the pattern it produced: a summary card plus a flag-and-count banner is one I'd now reach for on any results-heavy record, not just this one.
